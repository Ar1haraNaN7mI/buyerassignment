## Part One: Generative AI

### Core Issues With the Website

Generative AI was used first as a diagnostic assistant rather than as an autonomous strategist. The prompt asked the tool to evaluate Beyond Bouldering from the position of a first-time visitor who is interested in indoor bouldering but uncertain about safety, equipment, pricing, locations, and waiver requirements. This framing matters because the website does not operate only as a communication artefact; it is a pre-service decision environment. Lemon and Verhoef (2016) argue that customer experience is formed across multiple touchpoints in the customer journey, so the homepage should be evaluated by how well it prepares the visitor for the physical gym experience, not only by how visually exciting it appears.

The AI-generated diagnosis identified four connected problems: unclear beginner onboarding, fragmented pricing comparison, weak linkage between online browsing and waiver completion, and insufficient local/routine-based location guidance. These are not isolated usability defects. They represent a deeper business problem: the website does not fully convert latent interest into visit readiness. A user may be attracted to the brand but still abandon the journey if they cannot quickly answer basic decision questions. This aligns with cognitive load theory, which suggests that avoidable complexity consumes working memory and reduces effective problem solving (Sweller, 1988). In this case, the problem is not simply that information is absent; it is that the information must be sequenced around the user's decision process.

The four Assignment 2 issues can therefore be restated at a higher strategic level. First, the beginner experience requires stronger risk reduction because new users may associate climbing with danger, embarrassment, or specialist equipment. Second, the pricing system requires stronger decision architecture because each product option signals a different level of behavioural commitment. Third, the location experience requires stronger contextualisation because Adelaide users are likely to choose a gym according to commuting routines, suburb proximity, and after-work convenience. Fourth, the website requires stronger digital-to-physical continuity because waiver completion and arrival preparation are part of the same service encounter, not separate administrative tasks.

The AI-identified issues and the Assignment 2 issues overlap, but the critical evaluation refined their meaning. The AI was useful in identifying symptoms of friction; however, human judgement was needed to interpret these symptoms as commercial barriers. For example, "unclear pricing" is not only a content issue. It affects perceived value, purchase confidence, and commitment escalation. Similarly, "weak location information" is not only a navigation issue. It affects local SEO, market capture, and the user's ability to imagine the service fitting into daily routines. Online customer experience research shows that website experience influences trust, satisfaction, and behavioural outcomes (Rose et al., 2012), so these issues should be treated as strategic conversion risks.

The revised core issues used for the prototype are therefore: (1) first-time visitor uncertainty is not sufficiently reduced; (2) pricing options are not framed as decision-support choices; (3) location content does not fully exploit local intent and routine fit; and (4) waiver preparation is not integrated strongly enough into the pre-visit journey. These revised issues directly informed the website structure: the First Time section reduces perceived risk, the pricing cards support comparison, the location tabs translate branch choice into routine choice, the SEO section targets local search discovery, and the waiver form closes the digital-to-physical journey.

### Keywords

Generative AI was also used to generate an initial keyword universe. The suggested phrases included "bouldering for beginners in Adelaide," "indoor bouldering Adelaide CBD," "beginner climbing gym Kent Town," "climbing gym Keswick," "bouldering classes Adelaide," and "social fitness Adelaide." These phrases are analytically stronger than generic brand terms because they express problem recognition and local intent. Kannan and Li (2017) argue that digital marketing should be understood as a technology-enabled process connecting customers, firms, and touchpoints. In this context, keywords are not merely traffic devices; they are signals of the user's stage in the decision journey.

The keyword strategy should distinguish between three intent clusters. The first cluster is beginner intent, including terms such as "bouldering for beginners in Adelaide" and "beginner climbing gym." These users need reassurance and education before purchase. The second cluster is local convenience intent, including "indoor bouldering Adelaide CBD," "Kent Town climbing gym," and "climbing gym Keswick." These users may already understand the category but need a convenient location. The third cluster is progression or community intent, including "bouldering classes Adelaide," "private climbing coaching," and "social fitness Adelaide." These users may be more valuable over time because they are closer to repeat engagement.

The SEMrush keyword list should be inserted here after manual research and compared with the AI list using four criteria: search volume, keyword difficulty, commercial relevance, and journey fit. A high-volume generic term may be attractive but strategically weak if it attracts users outside Adelaide or users with low purchase intent. Conversely, a lower-volume suburb-specific keyword may have stronger conversion potential because it matches a user's immediate location constraint. Tiago and Veríssimo (2014) note that digital marketing requires firms to engage users in the channels and contexts where they actively search and compare; therefore, keyword selection should be judged by intent quality, not volume alone.

The critical keyword evaluation should also consider content placement. Beginner-intent terms belong in the First Time section, where the page explains safety, equipment, and arrival expectations. Location-intent terms belong in the Locations section, where the page compares Kent Town, Keswick, and Thebarton. Class and coaching terms belong in the Classes and Community section, where the website can move users from first trial to repeat participation. This prevents keyword stuffing and makes search optimisation part of service design. The final keyword set should therefore combine SEMrush-validated terms with AI-generated long-tail phrases, but only where they support the user journey.

**Final keyword statement:** The redesigned website should prioritise long-tail local intent rather than generic traffic. The selected keywords should combine beginner reassurance, Adelaide/suburb relevance, and progression-based class intent. The purpose of SEO is not simply to increase visits; it is to attract users whose search behaviour indicates readiness to evaluate, compare, and plan a real visit.
